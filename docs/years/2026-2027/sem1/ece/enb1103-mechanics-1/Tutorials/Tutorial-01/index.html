Tutorial 01: 3D Cartesian Vectors & Position Vectors

Fundamental concepts, 2D vs. 3D rectangular components, direction cosines, unit vectors, position vectors, and worked problems.
Published

November 4, 2026

1 Fundamental Quantities of Mechanics

Engineering mechanics relies on four basic quantities:

  • Length: Used to locate the position of a point in space and describe the size of a physical system; once a standard unit of length is defined, distances and geometric properties of a body are expressed as multiples of this unit.
  • Time: Conceived as a succession of events; while the principles of statics are time-independent, time plays an important role in dynamics.
  • Mass: A measure of a quantity of matter used to compare the action of one body with another; it manifests as gravitational attraction between two bodies and measures the resistance of matter to a change in velocity.
  • Force: Considered as a “push” or “pull” exerted by one body on another, occurring either through direct contact (such as pushing on a wall) or through a distance when physically separated (such as gravitational, electrical, and magnetic forces). A force is completely characterized by its magnitude, direction, and point of application.

2 From 2D to 3D Rectangular Components

2.1 2D Review vs. 3D Extension

In two dimensions, a vector \(\mathbf{A}\) making an angle \(\theta\) with the positive \(x\)-axis is resolved into rectangular components using simple right-triangle trigonometry:

\[A_x = A \cos\theta, \quad A_y = A \sin\theta\]

\[\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{} = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}, \quad \angle\theta = \tan^{-1}\!\left(\frac{A_y}{A_x}\right)\]

In three dimensions, vector algebra operations are greatly simplified by expressing vectors in Cartesian vector form.

2.2 Right-Handed Coordinate System

We use a right-handed coordinate system where the positive \(z\)-axis is directed upward (the zenith direction) to measure height or altitude, while the \(x\) and \(y\) axes lie in the horizontal plane. A rectangular coordinate system is right-handed if the thumb of the right hand points in the direction of the positive \(z\)-axis when the right-hand fingers are curled about this axis from the positive \(x\)-axis toward the positive \(y\)-axis.

WarningWatch Your Axis Orientation!

Swapping the positive \(x\) and \(y\) axes so that curling your fingers from \(+x\) to \(+y\) points your thumb downward creates a left-handed coordinate system, which is wrong for our vector formulations. Always verify your axes using the right-hand rule before reading coordinates.

2.3 Rectangular & Cartesian Vector Representation

When a vector \(\mathbf{A}\) is directed within an octant of the \(x, y, z\) frame, two successive applications of the parallelogram law resolve the vector first into \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}' + \mathbf{A}_z\) (where \(\mathbf{A}'\) is the projection in the \(xy\)-plane) and then \(\mathbf{A}' = \mathbf{A}_x + \mathbf{A}_y\), yielding the vector sum:

\[\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{A}_x + \mathbf{A}_y + \mathbf{A}_z\]

ImportantTA Note: Separate Magnitude and Direction

Writing \(A = A_x + A_y + A_z\) as a scalar sum is not accurate because each component has both a magnitude and a distinct axis direction. Using the Cartesian unit vectors \(\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}\) along the positive \(x, y, z\) axes separates the scalar magnitude (\(A_x, A_y, A_z\)) from the direction (\(\mathbf{i}, \mathbf{j}, \mathbf{k}\)):

\[\mathbf{A} = A_x\mathbf{i} + A_y\mathbf{j} + A_z\mathbf{k}\]

2.4 Magnitude of a Cartesian Vector

From the two right triangles formed by the 3D parallelepiped, \(A = \sqrt{A'^2 + A_z^2}\) and \(A' = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2}\). Combining these equations to eliminate \(A'\) gives the magnitude of \(\mathbf{A}\) as the positive square root of the sum of the squares of its components:

\[\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{} = A = \sqrt{A_x^2 + A_y^2 + A_z^2}\]

3 Direction Cosines & Unit Vectors

3.1 Coordinate Direction Angles (\(\alpha, \beta, \gamma\))

The direction of a Cartesian vector \(\mathbf{A}\) is defined by the coordinate direction angles \(\alpha\) (alpha), \(\beta\) (beta), and \(\gamma\) (gamma)—also abbreviated as \(\theta_x = \alpha\), \(\theta_y = \beta\), and \(\theta_z = \gamma\)—measured between the tail of \(\mathbf{A}\) and the positive \(x, y, z\) axes located at the tail of \(\mathbf{A}\). Regardless of where \(\mathbf{A}\) is directed, each angle lies between \(0^\circ\) and \(180^\circ\).

Projecting \(\mathbf{A}\) onto the \(x, y, z\) axes forms three right triangles that define the direction cosines of \(\mathbf{A}\):

\[\cos\alpha = \frac{A_x}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}}, \quad \cos\beta = \frac{A_y}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}}, \quad \cos\gamma = \frac{A_z}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}}\]

Note also that if the angle \(\gamma\) with the \(z\)-axis is given, the intermediate projection onto the \(xy\)-plane has magnitude \(A' = A\sin\gamma\).

3.2 Unit Vector (\(\mathbf{u}_A\)) and Direction Cosine Identity

Dividing \(\mathbf{A}\) by its magnitude \(\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}\) produces a dimensionless unit vector \(\mathbf{u}_A\) of magnitude \(1\) pointing in the direction of \(\mathbf{A}\):

\[\mathbf{u}_A = \frac{\mathbf{A}}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}} = \frac{A_x}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}}\mathbf{i} + \frac{A_y}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}}\mathbf{j} + \frac{A_z}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}}\mathbf{k} = \cos\alpha\,\mathbf{i} + \cos\beta\,\mathbf{j} + \cos\gamma\,\mathbf{k}\]

Because the magnitude of \(\mathbf{u}_A\) is equal to \(1\), the sum of the squares of the direction cosines satisfies the fundamental identity:

\[\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1\]

If any two of the coordinate direction angles are known, the third angle can be found using this equation. Furthermore, if the magnitude and coordinate direction angles of \(\mathbf{A}\) are known, \(\mathbf{A}\) is expressed in Cartesian vector form as:

\[\mathbf{A} = \vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}\cdot\mathbf{u}_A = A\cos\alpha\,\mathbf{i} + A\cos\beta\,\mathbf{j} + A\cos\gamma\,\mathbf{k}\]

4 Position Vectors & Force Along a Line

A position vector \(\mathbf{r}\) is a fixed vector that locates a point in space relative to another point:

  1. From the Origin (\(O\) to \(P\)): If \(\mathbf{r}\) extends from the origin of coordinates \(O(0,0,0)\) to a point \(P(x, y, z)\), then: \[\mathbf{r} = P - O = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k}\] For example, starting at \(O\), measuring \(x_A = +4\text{ m}\), \(y_A = +2\text{ m}\), and \(z_A = -6\text{ m}\) locates \(A(4\text{ m}, 2\text{ m}, -6\text{ m})\), while \(B(6\text{ m}, -1\text{ m}, 4\text{ m})\) is located at \(x_B = +6\text{ m}\), \(y_B = -1\text{ m}\), and \(z_B = +4\text{ m}\).
  2. Between Two Points (\(A\) to \(B\)): For a position vector directed from an initial point \(A(x_A, y_A, z_A)\) to a terminal point \(B(x_B, y_B, z_B)\) (or from \(P\) to \(Q\)), head-to-tail triangle addition requires \(\mathbf{r}_A + \mathbf{r}_{AB} = \mathbf{r}_B\), giving the difference between the two points (“destination minus origin”): \[\mathbf{r}_{AB} = \mathbf{r}_B - \mathbf{r}_A = (x_B - x_A)\mathbf{i} + (y_B - y_A)\mathbf{j} + (z_B - z_A)\mathbf{k}\]

To express a force \(\mathbf{F}\) of known magnitude \(F\) directed along a cable or line from \(A\) to \(B\), multiply the scalar magnitude \(F\) by the unit vector \(\mathbf{u}_{AB} = \frac{\mathbf{r}_{AB}}{\vert{}\mathbf{r}_{AB}\vert{}}\).

5 Worked Lecture Examples

5.1 Example 1: Force Directed Along a Cable

Problem: A cable attached to an eyebolt at \(A(4\text{ m}, 0, 3\text{ m})\) is pulled toward point \(B(0, 6\text{ m}, 0)\) with a force \(\mathbf{F}\) of magnitude \(500\text{ N}\). Determine the rectangular representation of this force.

Solution:

  1. Write the position vector \(\vec{AB}\) from \(A\) to \(B\) in rectangular form: Avoid common errors (choosing the wrong signs or mixing up scalar components) by sketching the parallelepiped from \(A\) to \(B\): \[\vec{AB} = -4\mathbf{i} + 6\mathbf{j} - 3\mathbf{k}\text{ m}\]

  2. Evaluate the unit vector \(\boldsymbol{\lambda} = \mathbf{u}_{AB}\) from \(A\) toward \(B\): \[\mathbf{u}_{AB} = \boldsymbol{\lambda} = \frac{\vec{AB}}{\vert{}\vec{AB}\vert{}} = \frac{-4\mathbf{i} + 6\mathbf{j} - 3\mathbf{k}}{\sqrt{(-4)^2 + 6^2 + (-3)^2}} = -0.5122\mathbf{i} + 0.7682\mathbf{j} - 0.3841\mathbf{k}\]

  3. Multiply by the magnitude of the force (\(F = 500\text{ N}\)): \[\mathbf{F} = F\boldsymbol{\lambda} = 500(-0.5122\mathbf{i} + 0.7682\mathbf{j} - 0.3841\mathbf{k}) = \{-256\mathbf{i} + 384\mathbf{j} - 192\mathbf{k}\}\text{ N}\]

5.2 Example 2: Force from Coordinate Direction Angles

Problem: Express the force \(F = 200\text{ N}\) acting in the first octant with \(\beta = 60^\circ\) and \(\gamma = 45^\circ\) as a Cartesian vector.

Solution:

  1. Find the unknown coordinate direction angle \(\alpha\): Using the direction cosine identity: \[\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1 \implies \cos^2\alpha + \cos^2 60^\circ + \cos^2 45^\circ = 1\]

    (Note: The lecture slide contains a typographical error printing \(\cos^2 0^\circ\) and \((0)^2\) in the intermediate substitution line instead of \(\cos^2 60^\circ = (0.5)^2\), though its final root \(\pm 0.5\) is based on \(\beta = 60^\circ\).) \[\cos\alpha = \sqrt{1 - (0.5)^2 - (0.707)^2} = \pm 0.5 \implies \alpha = \cos^{-1}(0.5) = 60^\circ \text{ or } \cos^{-1}(-0.5) = 120^\circ\]

    Because the force vector points in the positive \(x\) direction, we select \(\alpha = 60^\circ\).

  2. Express \(\mathbf{F}\) in Cartesian vector form: \[\mathbf{F} = F\cos\alpha\,\mathbf{i} + F\cos\beta\,\mathbf{j} + F\cos\gamma\,\mathbf{k}\]

    \[\mathbf{F} = (200\cos 60^\circ\text{ N})\mathbf{i} + (200\cos 60^\circ\text{ N})\mathbf{j} + (200\cos 45^\circ\text{ N})\mathbf{k} = \{100.0\mathbf{i} + 100.0\mathbf{j} + 141.4\mathbf{k}\}\text{ N}\]

5.3 Example 3: Double-Projection Resolution (\(A_{xy}\) Method)

Problem: For the position vector \(\mathbf{A}\) of magnitude \(A = 12\text{ m}\) making an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the positive \(z\)-axis and whose projection in the \(xy\)-plane makes an angle of \(40^\circ\) with the positive \(x\)-axis toward the positive \(y\)-axis, determine (1) the rectangular representation of \(\mathbf{A}\) and (2) the angles between \(\mathbf{A}\) and each of the positive coordinate axes.

Solution:

  1. Resolve \(\mathbf{A}\) into vertical (\(A_z\)) and horizontal (\(A_{xy}\)) components, then resolve \(A_{xy}\) into \(A_x\) and \(A_y\): \[A_z = A\cos 30^\circ = 12\cos 30^\circ = 10.392\text{ m}\]

    \[A_{xy} = A\sin 30^\circ = 12\sin 30^\circ = 6\text{ m}\]

    \[A_x = A_{xy}\cos 40^\circ = 6\cos 40^\circ = 4.596\text{ m}\]

    \[A_y = A_{xy}\sin 40^\circ = 6\sin 40^\circ = 3.857\text{ m}\]

    \[\mathbf{A} = A_x\mathbf{i} + A_y\mathbf{j} + A_z\mathbf{k} = \{4.60\mathbf{i} + 3.86\mathbf{j} + 10.39\mathbf{k}\}\text{ m}\]

  2. Calculate the coordinate direction angles (\(\theta_x, \theta_y, \theta_z\)): \[\theta_x = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{A_x}{A}\right) = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{4.596}{12}\right) = 67.5^\circ\]

    \[\theta_y = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{A_y}{A}\right) = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{3.857}{12}\right) = 71.3^\circ\]

    \[\theta_z = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{A_z}{A}\right) = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{10.392}{12}\right) = 30.0^\circ\]

5.4 Example 4: Obtuse Coordinate Direction Angles

Problem: Express the force \(F = 500\text{ N}\) (directed in the \(+x, -y, -z\) octant, making \(45^\circ\) with the negative \(y\)-axis and \(60^\circ\) with the negative \(z\)-axis) as a Cartesian vector.

Solution:

  1. Determine the coordinate direction angles from the positive axes: Since the vector makes \(45^\circ\) with \(-y\) and \(60^\circ\) with \(-z\), the supplementary angles measured from the positive \(y\) and \(z\) axes are \(\beta = 180^\circ - 45^\circ = 135^\circ\) and \(\gamma = 180^\circ - 60^\circ = 120^\circ\). \[\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2 135^\circ + \cos^2 120^\circ = 1 \implies \alpha = 60^\circ\]

  2. Write the Cartesian vector: \[\mathbf{F} = F\mathbf{u}_F = (500\text{ N})(0.5\mathbf{i} - 0.7071\mathbf{j} - 0.5\mathbf{k}) = \{250\mathbf{i} - 354\mathbf{j} - 250\mathbf{k}\}\text{ N}\]

6 Guided Tutorial Practice Problems (Problems 1–4)

6.1 Problem 1

Statement: Obtain the rectangular representation of the force \(\mathbf{P}\), given that its magnitude is \(30\text{ N}\), it is inclined at \(50^\circ\) above its projection in the \(xy\)-plane, and its \(xy\)-plane projection makes an angle of \(30^\circ\) with the \(+y\) axis toward the \(-x\) axis.

Solution:

  • Project \(\mathbf{P}\) onto the \(z\)-axis and the \(xy\)-plane: \[P_z = 30\sin 50^\circ = 22.98\text{ N} \approx 23\text{ N}\]

    \[P_{xy} = 30\cos 50^\circ = 19.28\text{ N}\]

  • Resolve \(P_{xy}\) along the \(-x\) and \(+y\) axes: \[P_x = -P_{xy}\sin 30^\circ = -19.28(0.5) = -9.64\text{ N}\] \[P_y = P_{xy}\cos 30^\circ = 19.28(0.8660) = 16.70\text{ N}\] \[\mathbf{P} = \{-9.64\mathbf{i} + 16.70\mathbf{j} + 22.98\mathbf{k}\}\text{ N}\]

NoteTA Caution on Slide Annotations

The handwritten annotation on the lecture slide lists \(P_x = -15\), \(P_y = 15\sqrt{3}\), and \(P_z = 23\). Notice that \(-15 = -30\sin 30^\circ\) and \(15\sqrt{3} = 30\cos 30^\circ\), which resolves the full \(30\text{ N}\) magnitude directly in the \(xy\)-plane without first multiplying by \(\cos 50^\circ\)! Always project onto the \(xy\)-plane (\(P_{xy} = P\cos 50^\circ\)) before resolving into \(x\) and \(y\) components.

6.2 Problem 2

Statement: The force \(\mathbf{F}\) acts on the bracket within the octant shown. If \(F = 400\text{ N}\), \(\beta = 60^\circ\), and \(\gamma = 45^\circ\), determine the \(x, y, z\) components of \(\mathbf{F}\).

Solution:

  • Using \(\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1\): \[\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2 60^\circ + \cos^2 45^\circ = 1 \implies \cos\alpha = \pm 0.5\]
  • Since \(\mathbf{F}\) is directed into the \(-x, +y, +z\) octant (\(\alpha = 120^\circ\)), \(\cos\alpha = -0.5\) (magnitude \(\vert{}F_x\vert{} = 200\text{ N}\)): \[F_x = -400(0.5) = -200\text{ N} \quad (\text{magnitude } 200\text{ N})\] \[F_y = 400\cos 60^\circ = 200\text{ N}\] \[F_z = 400\cos 45^\circ = 282.843\text{ N}\]

6.3 Problem 3

Statement: Determine the magnitude and coordinate direction angles of \(\mathbf{F}_2\) so that the resultant of the two forces (\(\mathbf{F}_1 = 180\text{ N}\) and \(\mathbf{F}_2\)) acts along the positive \(x\)-axis and has a magnitude of \(500\text{ N}\).

Solution:

  1. Resolve \(\mathbf{F}_1 = 180\text{ N}\) into components: \(\mathbf{F}_1\) is inclined \(15^\circ\) below the \(xy\)-plane along a projection line \(60^\circ\) from the \(+y\) axis toward the \(+x\) axis: \[F_{1x} = (180\cos 15^\circ)\sin 60^\circ = 150.57\text{ N}\] \[F_{1y} = (180\cos 15^\circ)\cos 60^\circ = 86.93\text{ N}\] \[F_{1z} = -180\sin 15^\circ = -46.59\text{ N}\]
  2. Apply \(\mathbf{F}_R = \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2 = 500\mathbf{i}\text{ N}\): \[F_{2x} = 500 - 150.57 = 349.43\text{ N}\] \[F_{2y} = 0 - 86.93 = -86.93\text{ N}\] \[F_{2z} = 0 - (-46.59) = 46.59\text{ N}\]
  3. Compute the magnitude and coordinate direction angles of \(\mathbf{F}_2\): \[F_2 = \sqrt{(349.43)^2 + (-86.93)^2 + (46.59)^2} = 363.1\text{ N}\] \[\alpha_2 = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{349.43}{363.1}\right) = 15.8^\circ, \quad \beta_2 = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{-86.93}{363.1}\right) = 103.9^\circ, \quad \gamma_2 = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{46.59}{363.1}\right) = 82.6^\circ\]

6.4 Problem 4

Statement: The pole is subjected to the force \(\mathbf{F}\) which has components \(F_x = 1.5\text{ kN}\) and \(F_z = 1.25\text{ kN}\). If \(\beta = 75^\circ\), determine the magnitudes of \(\mathbf{F}\) and \(F_y\).

Solution:

  • Express \(F_y\) in terms of \(F\): \(F_y = F\cos\beta = F\cos 75^\circ\).
  • Substitute into the magnitude relation \(F^2 = F_x^2 + F_y^2 + F_z^2\): \[F^2 = (1.5)^2 + (F\cos 75^\circ)^2 + (1.25)^2 \implies F^2(1 - \cos^2 75^\circ) = 3.8125\] \[F = \frac{\sqrt{3.8125}}{\sin 75^\circ} = 2.02\text{ kN}\] \[F_y = 2.021\cos 75^\circ = 0.523\text{ kN}\]

```{python}
def render_tikz(tikz_code: str, scale: float = 1.25) -> HTML:
    """Compiles a TikZ snippet to an inline SVG using latex + dvisvgm."""
    tex_doc = f"""\\documentclass[dvisvgm,tikz,border=6pt]{{standalone}}

        )
```

## Direction Cosines & Unit Vectors

### Coordinate Direction Angles ($\alpha, \beta, \gamma$)

The direction of a Cartesian vector $\mathbf{A}$ is defined by the **coordinate direction angles** $\alpha$ (alpha), $\beta$ (beta), and $\gamma$ (gamma)—also abbreviated as $\theta_x = \alpha$, $\theta_y = \beta$, and $\theta_z = \gamma$—measured between the tail of $\mathbf{A}$ and the positive $x, y, z$ axes located at the tail of $\mathbf{A}$. Regardless of where $\mathbf{A}$ is directed, each angle lies between $0^\circ$ and $180^\circ$.

Projecting $\mathbf{A}$ onto the $x, y, z$ axes forms three right triangles that define the **direction cosines** of $\mathbf{A}$:

$$\cos\alpha = \frac{A_x}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}}, \quad \cos\beta = \frac{A_y}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}}, \quad \cos\gamma = \frac{A_z}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}}$$

Note also that if the angle $\gamma$ with the $z$-axis is given, the intermediate projection onto the $xy$-plane has magnitude $A' = A\sin\gamma$.

### Unit Vector ($\mathbf{u}_A$) and Direction Cosine Identity

Dividing $\mathbf{A}$ by its magnitude $\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}$ produces a dimensionless **unit vector** $\mathbf{u}_A$ of magnitude $1$ pointing in the direction of $\mathbf{A}$:

$$\mathbf{u}_A = \frac{\mathbf{A}}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}} = \frac{A_x}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}}\mathbf{i} + \frac{A_y}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}}\mathbf{j} + \frac{A_z}{\vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}}\mathbf{k} = \cos\alpha\,\mathbf{i} + \cos\beta\,\mathbf{j} + \cos\gamma\,\mathbf{k}$$

Because the magnitude of $\mathbf{u}_A$ is equal to $1$, the sum of the squares of the direction cosines satisfies the fundamental identity:

$$\cos^2\alpha + \cos^2\beta + \cos^2\gamma = 1$$

If any two of the coordinate direction angles are known, the third angle can be found using this equation. Furthermore, if the magnitude and coordinate direction angles of $\mathbf{A}$ are known, $\mathbf{A}$ is expressed in Cartesian vector form as:

$$\mathbf{A} = \vert{}\mathbf{A}\vert{}\cdot\mathbf{u}_A = A\cos\alpha\,\mathbf{i} + A\cos\beta\,\mathbf{j} + A\cos\gamma\,\mathbf{k}$$

## Position Vectors & Force Along a Line

A **position vector** $\mathbf{r}$ is a fixed vector that locates a point in space relative to another point:

1. **From the Origin ($O$ to $P$):** If $\mathbf{r}$ extends from the origin of coordinates $O(0,0,0)$ to a point $P(x, y, z)$, then:
   $$\mathbf{r} = P - O = x\mathbf{i} + y\mathbf{j} + z\mathbf{k}$$
   For example, starting at $O$, measuring $x_A = +4\text{ m}$, $y_A = +2\text{ m}$, and $z_A = -6\text{ m}$ locates $A(4\text{ m}, 2\text{ m}, -6\text{ m})$, while $B(6\text{ m}, -1\text{ m}, 4\text{ m})$ is located at $x_B = +6\text{ m}$, $y_B = -1\text{ m}$, and $z_B = +4\text{ m}$.
2. **Between Two Points ($A$ to $B$):** For a position vector directed from an initial point $A(x_A, y_A, z_A)$ to a terminal point $B(x_B, y_B, z_B)$ (or from $P$ to $Q$), head-to-tail triangle addition requires $\mathbf{r}_A + \mathbf{r}_{AB} = \mathbf{r}_B$, giving the difference between the two points ("destination minus origin"):
   $$\mathbf{r}_{AB} = \mathbf{r}_B - \mathbf{r}_A = (x_B - x_A)\mathbf{i} + (y_B - y_A)\mathbf{j} + (z_B - z_A)\mathbf{k}$$

To express a force $\mathbf{F}$ of known magnitude $F$ directed along a cable or line from $A$ to $B$, multiply the scalar magnitude $F$ by the unit vector $\mathbf{u}_{AB} = \frac{\mathbf{r}_{AB}}{\vert{}\mathbf{r}_{AB}\vert{}}$.

1. **Resolve $\mathbf{F}_1 = 180\text{ N}$ into components:** $\mathbf{F}_1$ is inclined $15^\circ$ below the $xy$-plane along a projection line $60^\circ$ from the $+y$ axis toward the $+x$ axis:
   $$F_{1x} = (180\cos 15^\circ)\sin 60^\circ = 150.57\text{ N}$$
   $$F_{1y} = (180\cos 15^\circ)\cos 60^\circ = 86.93\text{ N}$$
   $$F_{1z} = -180\sin 15^\circ = -46.59\text{ N}$$
2. **Apply $\mathbf{F}_R = \mathbf{F}_1 + \mathbf{F}_2 = 500\mathbf{i}\text{ N}$:**
   $$F_{2x} = 500 - 150.57 = 349.43\text{ N}$$
   $$F_{2y} = 0 - 86.93 = -86.93\text{ N}$$
   $$F_{2z} = 0 - (-46.59) = 46.59\text{ N}$$
3. **Compute the magnitude and coordinate direction angles of $\mathbf{F}_2$:**
   $$F_2 = \sqrt{(349.43)^2 + (-86.93)^2 + (46.59)^2} = 363.1\text{ N}$$
   $$\alpha_2 = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{349.43}{363.1}\right) = 15.8^\circ, \quad \beta_2 = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{-86.93}{363.1}\right) = 103.9^\circ, \quad \gamma_2 = \cos^{-1}\!\left(\frac{46.59}{363.1}\right) = 82.6^\circ$$

### Problem 4
**Statement:** The pole is subjected to the force $\mathbf{F}$ which has components $F_x = 1.5\text{ kN}$ and $F_z = 1.25\text{ kN}$. If $\beta = 75^\circ$, determine the magnitudes of $\mathbf{F}$ and $F_y$.

```{python}
#| echo: false
render_tikz(r"""
\begin{tikzpicture}[x={(-0.55cm,-0.42cm)}, y={(1cm,0cm)}, z={(0cm,1cm)}, scale=0.85, >=stealth, line cap=round, line join=round, font=\small]

  % Resultant Vector F
  \draw[->, ultra thick, cyan!70!blue] (0,0,0) -- (2.6,2.2,2.3) node[above right] {$\mathbf{F}$};

  \draw[->, red!75!black, thick] (0,1.4,0) to[bend right=20] node[right, font=\footnotesize] {$\beta = 75^\circ$} (1.0,0.85,0.88);
```

**Solution:**

* Express $F_y$ in terms of $F$: $F_y = F\cos\beta = F\cos 75^\circ$.
* Substitute into the magnitude relation $F^2 = F_x^2 + F_y^2 + F_z^2$:
  $$F^2 = (1.5)^2 + (F\cos 75^\circ)^2 + (1.25)^2 \implies F^2(1 - \cos^2 75^\circ) = 3.8125$$
  $$F = \frac{\sqrt{3.8125}}{\sin 75^\circ} = 2.02\text{ kN}$$
  $$F_y = 2.021\cos 75^\circ = 0.523\text{ kN}$$
